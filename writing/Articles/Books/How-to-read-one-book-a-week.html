How to Read One Book a Week

Reading more isn't primarily about reading faster. It's about building an environment, system and identity that makes books a natural part of your life.

Most people say they want to read more. They buy books, create reading lists and occasionally set ambitious goals for the year.

But the problem usually isn't a lack of books. It's that reading has to compete with things specifically designed to capture our attention: social media, videos, messages, news and endless entertainment.

If you want to read a book every week, the answer probably isn't forcing yourself to sit down for several hours a day. A better approach is to redesign your environment and habits so that reading becomes the default activity during the small gaps already present in your day.

Here are nine principles that can make that easier.

1. Always have a book available

The first rule is simple: make sure a book is always within reach.

If you have five unexpected minutes while waiting somewhere, what do you reach for? For most people, the answer is their phone.

The phone itself isn't necessarily the problem. The problem is that it contains an entire universe competing for your attention.

Keep a physical book in your bag, an ebook on your phone or tablet, or an audiobook ready to play.

You don't need to create time for every page. You can capture the small pieces of time that already exist.

2. Use different formats for different situations

A paperback, ebook and audiobook are not competitors. They are tools for different environments.

A physical book might be ideal for focused reading at home. An ebook might work during a commute or while waiting somewhere. An audiobook can turn walking, driving or household chores into reading time.

The goal isn't to be loyal to one format. The goal is to reduce friction.

When the format matches the situation, reading becomes easier to fit into your life.

3. Stop forcing yourself to finish every book

One of the strangest rules people impose on themselves is the belief that starting a book creates an obligation to finish it.

Sometimes a book is simply not useful to you. Maybe it's poorly written. Maybe you've already understood the main idea. Maybe it was interesting when you bought it but no longer matches your current interests.

There is no prize for finishing a book you stopped caring about.

Reading should be selective. Your time and attention are limited resources. Learn to abandon books that aren't giving you enough value and move toward the ones that genuinely pull you forward.

4. Reduce the competition for your attention

Reading is cognitively more demanding than scrolling. That means if both options are available with equal convenience, scrolling usually wins.

The solution isn't necessarily greater willpower. It's changing the environment.

Remove or reduce the easiest distractions. Make your reading app easy to access. Keep a book visible. Don't expect yourself to make the difficult choice hundreds of times every day.

Good systems make the desired behavior easier and the unwanted behavior slightly more inconvenient.

5. Create a reading ritual before bed

One of the simplest places to build a reading habit is at the end of the day.

Even fifteen or twenty minutes can accumulate surprisingly fast. More importantly, attaching reading to an existing routine removes the question of when you will do it.

You don't need to negotiate with yourself every evening. Brushing your teeth happens. Getting into bed happens. Reading can become part of the same sequence.

The habit becomes stronger when it has a predictable trigger.

6. Build an antilibrary

Most people think of an impressive library as a collection of books they have already completed.

But there is another useful way to think about it: your unread books represent everything you still don't know.

Keep a growing list of books you want to explore. Let yourself be surrounded by interesting possibilities.

An unread library isn't evidence of failure. It can be a reminder that your knowledge is still incomplete and that there are always more ideas worth exploring.

7. Read in projects, not just individual books

Instead of randomly jumping from one interesting book to another, try creating reading projects.

Choose a subject you want to understand deeply: business, psychology, writing, history, relationships or any other area that matters to you.

Then read several books around that subject.

This creates something important: context. One book gives you one person's perspective. Five books allow you to compare ideas, notice patterns and identify disagreements.

Knowledge becomes more useful when ideas begin connecting with one another.

8. Talk about what you read

Reading becomes more valuable when it enters your conversations.

Discuss an idea with a friend. Write a short note. Explain a concept to someone else. Add your own thoughts and disagreements.

This does two things.

First, it helps you remember what you read. Second, it turns reading from passive consumption into something you actively use.

The real value of a book isn't finishing the last page. It's what happens after you close it.

9. Become someone who reads

This might be the most important principle of all.

Sustainable habits are often supported by identity.

Instead of constantly asking, "Do I feel like reading today?" you begin to see reading as something that is simply part of who you are.

You are a reader.

Readers read when they have time. They carry books. They become curious about subjects. They collect ideas. They abandon books that aren't useful and move on to better ones.

The behavior reinforces the identity, and the identity reinforces the behavior.

The bigger idea

Reading one book a week isn't really about hitting the number fifty-two.

The number is just a useful target.

The deeper objective is to build a life where books are a regular source of ideas, knowledge and perspective.

If you always have something interesting to read, reduce distractions, allow yourself to abandon bad books, read around specific subjects and gradually develop the identity of a reader, the number of books you finish will likely increase as a consequence.

Don't obsess over reading faster.

Build a system that makes reading happen more often.
